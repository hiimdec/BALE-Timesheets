Commercials and long form: what changes when you leave the APA terms

TimeMachine · Published 24 August 2026

Plenty of crew work both. You do a week of commercials, then eight weeks on a drama, and the money works in a completely different way, on a completely different document.

The APA Recommended Terms cover commercials. Long-form film and television run on agreements between Pact and Bectu instead. Same crew, same trucks, often the same stages, entirely separate rulebooks.

Three documents, not two

The first thing to understand is that these are not variations on one agreement. They were negotiated separately, by different parties, for different kinds of production, and they do not share definitions. A term that means one thing on a commercial can mean something different on a drama.

There are three, not two, because long form is itself split:

  • Commercials: the APA Recommended Terms for Engaging Crew on the Production of Commercials.
  • Scripted television: the Pact and Bectu agreement for scripted TV, effective 1 January 2023, which applies across four budget bands.
  • Feature film: the Pact and Bectu terms for major motion pictures, as amended 5 April 2021. These apply to a feature for cinema, or a single piece of theatric-type content for a global streaming platform, with a budget of £30 million or more. Below that threshold they do not apply.

So the instinct to carry your commercials knowledge across is the thing to resist. Check the agreement that governs the job you are actually on.

The day is shaped differently, but not where you would expect

Here is the part most people get backwards. A scripted TV shooting day is the same shape as a commercials one: ten hours worked plus an hour for lunch, eleven hours elapsed, overtime after eleven. If you move from commercials to television, the basic day does not move.

Film is the one that changes. A shooting day on a major motion picture is eleven hours worked plus an hour for lunch, so twelve hours elapsed, and overtime does not start until twelve.

The continuous working day splits the other way:

APA, commercials10 + 1, OT after 11h9 hoursBDR ÷ 10
Pact/Bectu, scripted TV10 + 1, OT after 11h9 hours, 20 min restBDR ÷ 10
Pact/Bectu, film11 + 1, OT after 12h10 hours, no breakweekly ÷ 55, daily ÷ 11

That last column is the one that catches people. On commercials and on television your hourly rate is a tenth of your day rate. On a film it is your weekly rate divided by 55, or your daily divided by 11, so the same headline figure produces a different hour and therefore a different overtime rate.

Overtime itself is graded on commercials, at 1.5, 1.25 or 1.0 depending on where your day rate sits. It is not graded on either long-form agreement. Scripted TV pays time and a half, with a floor and a ceiling on the overtime rate. Film separates camera overtime, at double, from non-camera overtime, at time and a half.

Length of engagement changes everything

The deeper difference is structural. Commercials are short. A job is a few days, each day largely stands alone, and you invoice for it. Nothing in the APA terms counts how many days in a row you have worked.

Long form is a run, and both long-form agreements price the run rather than the day. Work a sixth consecutive day and it is paid at time and a half on either agreement. A seventh is double. Scripted TV goes further and prohibits an eighth consecutive day outright. On film, a sixth or seventh day carries a minimum guarantee of eight hours if you are shooting.

Travel is where the two long-form agreements agree with each other and disagree with instinct: a travel day does not count towards the consecutive day tally on either. On film, a travel day under an official movement order does not even reset the count, and is not paid as a sixth or seventh day.

Turnaround exists on all three, at eleven hours, but what a broken one is worth differs. On commercials the cut hour is paid on top of the day. On scripted TV a broken turnaround is compensatory rest or an uplift, and if you take the rest there is no payment. On film it is time and a half, capped.

How you are engaged

Commercials crew are typically engaged directly and invoice the production company, which is why the APA terms carry a payment term at all: seven days from receipt for crew contracted directly.

Long form routes through a payroll either way. The scripted TV agreement sets out both routes explicitly: on PAYE, the week's or day's basic pay comes normally a week in arrears, with overtime and the other variables following within 14 days of the producer receiving written evidence of what you are claiming. Through a personal service company, you invoice, and you are paid on the next processed payroll after a valid and undisputed invoice.

If you are moving between the two, that is the thing to check before the job rather than after: who is paying you, on what basis, and what the payslip will actually look like.
